A system that grows food on its own, on whatever you already have

KrattOS is not a gadget you buy and plug in. It is the brain that turns a
shelf, a garden bed, or a greenhouse into a system that grows food by
itself. You give it water, power, and your kitchen scraps. It runs the
lights, mixes the nutrients, waters the plants, and keeps itself safe. You
walk up and pick food.

The whole point is that it adapts to what you already have. Clip it onto a
wire rack in a closet. Run it over raised beds in a yard. Drop it into a
hoop house. Same software, any shape, any budget. You bring the space.
KrattOS brings the brain.

And it runs entirely on its own. No subscription. No cloud account. No
internet connection, ever.


How it actually works

(Make the next four lines a bullet list.)
Hydroponic watering, so you never water by hand. Pumps and probes feed the
plants on a schedule the system sets and adjusts on its own.
A composter turns your kitchen scraps into the liquid nutrient that feeds
the plants. The loop closes: waste goes in, food comes out, nothing is
hauled away.
A local AI watches the plants through a camera, follows the recipe for
each crop, and decides when to light, water, and feed.
A separate hardware safety system can cut the power on its own if anything
goes wrong. The thinking part of the system can never override the part
that keeps it safe.

Why it should exist

Fresh food is getting further out of reach. Produce is expensive, it
travels thousands of miles to wilt on a shelf, and the people who most
need good nutrition are the ones with the least time, space, and money to
chase it. A working garden should not require a backyard, a flexible
schedule, or money to spare.

There is also a whole world of people who genuinely cannot put grow gear on
the internet: schools and restaurants that worry about data, labs and
clinics with biosecurity rules, remote sites and base kitchens, and plenty
of households that simply do not want another appliance reporting on what
they eat.

KrattOS is built for all of them. It is serious food equipment under the
hood, an AI brain, real-time controllers, a closed-loop hydroponic system,
and a hardware safety supervisor, but it bends to fit whatever space and
budget you bring to it.


Why the name

In Estonian folklore, a Kratt is a little household servant. You build it
out of whatever is lying around, straw, old tools, a broken pot, and it
comes to life to do the chores you would rather not. It is the right name
for a system that grows your food out of water, electricity, and your own
kitchen scraps, on whatever you have on hand.

KrattOS is the software that runs the modern Kratt.

Common questions

Is this just an indoor fridge thing?
No. The fridge-sized cabinet is one way to run KrattOS, not the point. The
software adapts to whatever you have: a wire shelf, a row of raised beds, a
greenhouse. You pick the body, KrattOS runs it. The cheapest setup clips
onto a rack you already own for around $149.

Will it actually grow enough food to matter?
Yes. Set up for leafy greens, a four-shelf KrattOS gives you roughly 4 to 6
heads of lettuce a week on rotation. That is about $25 to $40 of grocery
value a week depending on where you live. Scaled up to beds or a
greenhouse, it covers a lot more.

Why no internet?
Three reasons. Privacy: your kitchen should not be a data feed.
Reliability: a grower that bricks when a subscription lapses is a bad deal.
And many of the people who want this most, like hospital food services and
biosecurity labs, simply cannot put a connected device in their food
chain. Build for them first and everyone else benefits too.

What if the goal is not met?
GoFundMe lets you keep what you raise, so every dollar still moves this
forward. Even $400 builds the phase 1 unit and gets real greens growing on
camera. The full $3,000 funds the AI upgrade on top, which is the part
that proves the whole idea.

What if the build hits a wall?
The software already works. The risk is in the physical assembly, and at
this budget it is small: one unit on a rack, not a factory. If part of the
build proves harder than expected, everything learned gets published and
the kit pre-orders get refunded. The thank-you and seed tiers ship
regardless.

Is it open source?
The firmware, control loops, and update system are open. The recipe
library and the fleet software are commercial. That mix lets a hardware
project stay alive without quietly selling your data down the line.

since this is not a registered non-profit.
